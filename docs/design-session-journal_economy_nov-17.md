# Travelling Inn: Economy Session Status (Nov 17)

Design session on economy and suppliers, before progression. It sets a baseline for cost and reward, which keeper traits and progression pacing then build on. Follows the Nov 15 game start session.

## Decided

### Coin pays the bills, and people give it meaning

Money buys things. It never buys relationships or knowledge. Three ledgers stay apart: **coin** (spent), **reputation** (earned, never spent; decides how many come and which crowd) and **knowledge** (familiarity, tastes, recipe stars, discoveries; never lost).

### Where coin comes from

Guests always pay for what they ordered, since an ordered serve can't go wrong. Tips follow the reaction: a Delight tips well, Pleased a little, a miss nothing. Tips stay modest, so a Delight's real reward is the card, the familiarity and the room noticing.

### Unasked serves are on the house

Putting ingredients at risk on a guess is the hidden-needs pillar as an economy. A Delight pays it back in tips, familiarity and cards. Card Offers work the same way.

### Money: penny, shilling, crown

Three coins, ten of each to the next: 10d = 1s, 10s = 1 crown. Each coin covers a range: drinks in pennies, weekly costs in shillings, big equipment in crowns. (The real 12-and-20 ratios were too fiddly.)

### Prices never inflate

A pint is 4d on day one and 4d in year five. Seasonal produce can wobble a little. Earnings grow from more guests, better dishes, specialities and tips, never from bigger numbers.

### Prices: a default, one knob, a cover charge

- **Default:** each dish's price comes from its ingredients plus prep. The player never has to fiddle.
- **One house-wide knob:** Cheap, Fair or Dear, to steer the crowd.
- **Cover charge** for music nights and other events.
- **Out:** setting each dish's price yourself. Too fine-grained next to the others.

### Upkeep is something you can see

Upkeep is what it costs to keep the house open, whatever you sell. Every line is visible in the room, never an abstract drain.

- **Licence (or rent):** one weekly cost, whatever the story calls it. Weekly, so there's never a huge sum looming on the horizon.
- **Fuel:** wood or coal for hearth and stove. Follows the weather; the woodpile shrinks.
- **Light:** candles, lamp oil, lamps and the like. Follows the daylight, so winter costs more.
- **Sundries:** napkins, soap, matches, washing soda. One pantry crate that empties as you serve. Nobody counts napkins.
- **Wear:** chipped mugs, the odd broken plate, tied to messiness. This is where the fun is.

### Supplies are ordered item by item

You build your own order, a corn cob at a time. Suppliers *offer* bundles as specials: a discount, a seasonal crate, or the starter outfit.

### The order slip never gets lost

Your order is a pencilled slip waiting on the desk. Leave the screen, serve someone, sleep on it: it's still there. **Tear it up** throws it away. **Copy last week's slip** is the standing order. One slip per supplier.

### Each supplier delivers on their own days

At checkout you get the next date that supplier can do. Fetching it yourself is quicker and gets you face time and discoveries, at the cost of your time. A travelling salesman can be an occasional event, not a system.

### Deliveries have a place, and they pile up

Crates arrive at a delivery spot by the kitchen door and stack up there visibly. A growing pile is a nudge, never a penalty.

### Crates spoil slowly

Nothing ages on delivery day, so a busy morning costs nothing. From the next day, crate contents follow the normal clock (otherwise the crate becomes a free icebox). Spoiled food is not thrown away for you: it stays, marked, until you clear it into the leftover routes.

### Nobody misses a delivery

The carter arrives in-world each morning (cart, bell, knock), and the crate on the step stays visible until carried in. Supported by crate icons on the date strip, a "Put away the delivery" line on the morning checklist, an "Expected today" note on the desk or newspaper, the date in big type on a signed slip, and a top notice. Day two teaches it once, and sets up the first daily standing order (milk and bread).

### Shelf life has an "on the turn" stage

Before spoiling, food goes *on the turn*: still usable, it loses "fresh" and opens use-it-up recipes (sour milk to scones, stale bread to bread pudding). The fast-spoiling things are the cheap daily ones, so a standing order looks after them.

### Ready-made is mass, not bad

Bought goods are consistent but shallow: one or two broad tags, pleasant for most and a Delight for almost nobody. Your own dishes carry as many tags as you have ingredient slots. **Tags are the measure of quality.**

### Small joys never put the oven at risk

Records and decorations are cheap enough to buy on a whim, and they matter: they feed the room's feel (mood, how long people stay, what they order).

### Limited cooking, never barred

The small inn can cook different things early. The hearth is a second station (a bake kettle in the embers), and secondhand equipment is cheaper than new.

### Equipment you get to know

Use a piece of equipment long enough and something sticks: never random, never a drawback, not necessarily a flavour. A feel tag (the old range makes things *comforting*) or a working trait (dishes stay warm longer, the pot gives nine portions). You discover your stove's character the way you discover a guest's tastes.

### Charming breakdowns

Most of the time a whack fixes it. Every so often it needs the tinker.

### Cookware is the upgrade language

You buy the stove once. Pots, pans and skillets are what you collect, upgrade and season: a bigger stew pot, a griddle pan, a seasoned skillet.

### Scripted dialogue pauses the game

The tinker, the inspector and story beats pause everything; nobody loses a customer to a talky visitor. Small talk, card plays and greetings stay live. **Amends outline rule 6:** "small talk never stops the room".

### The tinker comes later, and teaches equipment

He turns up a few days in, before anything can break; breakdowns only start once he's been. The dialogue fires on his **second** serve (the first is crammy). If he's only served once, it fires as he gets up to leave, so the visit always happens.

### The catalogue is for gear, the locals are alive

Inspired by the Sears, Roebuck & Co. catalogue. The catalogue (city wholesaler) sells equipment, cookware, wholesale and technical goods, with colourful copy and black-and-white engraved art. Local suppliers are living people: you get to know them and build a working relationship.

## Proposed

### Baseline numbers (placeholders, in pennies)

| Item | Cost | Price at Fair | Margin |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Pint of ale | 2d | 4d | 2d |
| Cup of tea | 1d | 3d | 2d |
| Bowl of stew (pot of 8 costs 16d) | 2d | 6d | 4d |
| Bought pie | 5d | 7d | 2d |
| Snack bowl (free, makes guests thirsty) | 3d | — | Returns through drinks |

A typical guest spends about 12d and leaves about 6d. Upkeep: licence 14s a week (about 2s a day), fuel about 10d a day. A settled day of about 20 guests nets about 9s after upkeep; the first week, nearer 4s. Start with about 2 crowns: a week's restock plus one small buy.

### Pacing targets

These matter more than the prices.

- A small buy every 1 to 3 days: a record about 3s, the copper kettle 15s.
- A medium buy each week: a second hob, a used oven, a permit application.
- A big milestone each calendar season: a new oven about 12 crowns, a kitchen expansion about 20. A 28-day calendar season nets about 25 crowns.

The ladder for cooking: a bake kettle in the first days, a used oven in about a week, a new range in about a season.

The starting location changes who's strong: dockside gets fish, the mountainside dairy and game.

### Catalogue ideas

Good / Better / Best tiers on one page (the humble / middle / grand equipment table). Engraved one-ink art instead of sprites. An order blank you fill in and sign, which is the carter's clipboard. A new edition each calendar season, the winter one the Wish Book. Starter outfits as bundles. Earnest copy and testimonials that can hint at tastes. Dog-eared pages and pencil circles as the wishlist. Kit-house floor plans for the empty-plot stretch goal.

### New card effect: Act on the room

Two cards from the session don't fit Spec 4's vocabulary: **Clean Room** (cleans everything in the room) and **Jury Rig** (fixes broken equipment, maybe only until the tinker looks at it). Proposed: add one deliberate effect, *Act on the room*, for cleaning and repairs. Cards can also touch cookware (Love & Care).

### The tip jar

A jar on the counter, labelled by the player: the record fund, helping others, the ferryman's new boat. Guests give more to causes they care about. An upgrade or a Perk, depending on how Perks are structured.

### Perk: seasoned cookware

Cast iron picks up a signature tag with use.

## Ideas to develop

### Table snacks do more than make people thirsty (Nov 18)

Snacks are a small, cheap lever with several effects. Salty snacks already make people thirsty. More effects, one or two per snack:

- **Patience:** a snack a guest likes makes them more patient while they wait. It only works if they like it, so it rewards knowing your guests.
- **Hunger:** some snacks whet the appetite, making food orders more likely.
- **Coziness:** improves the room's feel for that table's guests only.
- **Priming:** a snack changes how dishes at that table taste. A wedge of lime before something sweet makes it read as brighter; something salty softens a bitter ale. You can set a snack on purpose to prime a table for a dish, or to rescue a soup that isn't great.

Priming must always be visible: the snack bowl shows its effect, and the dish shows the shifted tag (for example *sweet → bright*). Decided: priming lasts as long as the bowl has something in it. No wearing off, nothing to track: a filled bowl means every serve at that table is primed, an empty one means none. Proposed: it shifts or softens one tag per serve, never more. Ties to Spec 2 scoring and to room feel.

### How snacks are bought

Open. Proposed: snacks come from the general store by the jar or sack (bulk is the natural way to buy them), with the odd bundle as a special. One jar fills many bowls, and dry snacks keep, so they never spoil on you. Fresh ones (lime wedges, pickled eggs once opened) follow the shelf-life rules.

### Leftovers always go somewhere

The keeper's supper (may refill a card), the stock pot, pies, a day-old basket sold at closing, the inn dog, hens and the pig bin, compost for a kitchen garden, preserving (salt, pickle, smoke, jam) until the icebox. Forget the bin and the room smells: it hits the room's feel, not your purse. Needs its own session on how reuse is implemented.
