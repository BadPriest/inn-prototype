# Travelling Inn: Game Start Session Status (Nov 15)

Voice design session on Kanzo #36 (game start and progression). Feeds #7, #11 and #8.

## Decided

### Medium is a debug start

The medium tavern stays only as a test bench for systems a small start can't reach (oven, pies, bigger rooms). Players never see it.

### Permits are how the inn grows

Modifying the floor plan through permits is the main growth path. Permits are horizontal: the player gathers resources and applies for whichever one they like, in any order.

### You pay for the privilege of trying

The fee buys an application, not the permit. A bureaucracy comedy bit. The inspector's visit is the real exam.

### The start is very small

Two four-seat tables (down from two six-seaters), a small fireplace, very little equipment.

### Day one is a prologue

An intimate cutscene: the keeper and a friend talk over a drink in the tiny inn about having just got the place. The conversation carries the story and possibly the tutorial; the keeper gets up to serve. Turning in that night is the real start of the game. Next morning the keeper opens alone.

Tutorial shape (Nov 15, afternoon): on the first night the friend introduces cards and also hears about the delivery coming tomorrow, where the player will choose things ("I've a delivery coming tomorrow." "Ah yeah? What is it?"). The cards tutorial could then come at turn-in or on waking. Open: this brings the card tutorial earlier than the Cards section below says (after a long stretch of plain service); which holds?

### Day two opens with a delivery

A supplier brings the ordered crate in a cutscene, and the player picks a keg (first one free). The crate holds pantry supplies for one stove dish, one or two dishes the keeper cooks, a few drink garnishes and a few snacks.

### Bundles give the first choice

Suppliers offer bundles (not everything is bundled). At the start you pick among bundles; how many depends on their size and scope, from kitchen-only to kitchen and taproom. This lets the player shape the inn from day one without long menus.

### Few knobs at first

In the long run the player shapes everything: hours, music, activities, which patrons are welcome. Day two exposes only a few; the rest open up over time.

## Proposed starter set

Keeper's quarters: bed, desk, wardrobe. Inn: two four-seat tables, small fireplace, the gramophone, a one-hob stove with no oven, a kettle or a coffee pot, one keg (chosen), and the initial pantry. No prep table: it comes with the kitchen expansion. Answers #11 in draft; supersedes the earlier wood-fired oven start.

The one hob decides the day: when it's empty and clean, you choose what it's for, a stew or quick dishes (scrambled eggs, toast and the like), never both at once. Growth is your choice of upgrade: add an oven, more hobs, and so on (ties to #8).

The small tavern must be viable with no upgrades at all. Stove upgrades are bought on their own and never need a permit or a layout change. The kitchen's spare room is rented by another tenant as storage, so expanding the kitchen means getting that room back.

## Ideas to develop

### The friend gives the gramophone

A keepsake from the first night, so the music carries the friend with it.

### Inspectors as patrons

The inspector could be a patron with hidden tastes, and a Delight wins the permit. The first visit could teach how Delight works. Details of the visit still open.

### Reputation as word of mouth

No reputation system exists yet. Bruno enjoys growing a place from nothing into somewhere that means something, and reputation should express that. It builds from consistently good service; happy guests call their friends.

### Crowds you didn't intend

Word of mouth brings crowds that may not be the ones you want: you focus on stews, they just come to drink. Steer them indirectly (bill of fare, hours, music, prices) or directly.

### Door policies, as satire

A direct lever like banning a race ("humans only") is possible, played as satire with consequences: a pro-goblin supplier drops you. Not racism mechanics.

### Townsfolk loyalties (maybe)

Suppliers, inspectors and patrons have loyalties your choices bump into. Just a seed.

### Keeper creation

Still planned (Travellers Rest style), as the natural place to pick traits and kitchen skills that set starting recipes. Fine for now, though "rote". Not in the prototype.

## The first morning, step by step

1. **Delivery.** The delivery guy has you sign for a bundle and pick a free keg. You have some starting money; extra orders get a delivery date. He leaves.
2. **Unpack and place.** The crate sits in the kitchen and the keg needs installing. This teaches placement: everything is placeable, so the layout is both how you work and how the inn looks.
3. **Set the bill of fare.** Nudged before opening, so players don't learn bad habits. It lists every dish, with the ones you can't make clearly marked by what's missing (ingredients or skill), plus filters for both. A pantry tab shows what's on hand, and a missing ingredient links straight to ordering it.
4. **Read the newspaper (optional).** More than the weather: small stories that hint at local tastes and useful news.
5. **Prep the long dishes.** Stew pot on the stove, kettle on, coffee pot going, so they're ready when guests order.
6. **Open the door.**

## Suppliers

### The delivery scene hands off to the supplier screen

On day two the delivery guy, clipboard in hand, has you sign for the bundle and pick a free keg. Then he asks whether you'd like anything else delivered on another day, which opens the full supplier interface.

### You start as a reseller

At first there is little or no production: no brewing, no butchering bulk meat, maybe no dough unless the keeper is a baker. Suppliers sell everything: drinks, garnishes, snacks, ingredients, ready-made bread and pies, firewood, gramophone records.

### Prices challenge, never gouge

The game isn't out to bankrupt you, but money should take some striving. The balance is to be found, without tipping into too much simulation.

### Bought goods make you want to make your own

Ready-made food is a deliberate trade-off: mediocre quality, guests tire of the flavour, no customization. That itch pushes production in-house where the player cares. Some things, like bread, are fine to keep buying and turn into toast or sandwiches.

### A network of small businesses

The keeper builds business relationships with suppliers, not friendships. The baker is a small business too, and the inn becomes one node in a local network. Goal: suppliers also come in as patrons, so the network has faces.

### Bathrooms (maybe)

Patron bathrooms, to relieve the thirst need. Under consideration.

## Cards

### The friend plants cards on the first night

The keeper and the friend play cards over their drink, which puts cards in the player's mind without a lesson. The friend leaves the keeper one or two cards.

### Basics first, cards as modifiers

The first days teach serving with no cards involved. Cards modify the basics, so their tutorial comes after the basics are learned.

### Experiment first, explain later

The player can try the friend's cards freely from the start. A proper card tutorial comes later, after a long stretch of plain service.

### The friend's cards: A Kind Word, maybe Love & Care

A Kind Word sets the tone. A second could be something like Love & Care, spent on dishes and cookpots. Maybe the friend gives one card of each type. Parked: the card mechanic isn't ripe yet.

Open: what triggers the card tutorial (days played, a first Delight, a new card earned).

## More ideas from the session

### The keeper eats what they make

Cook a stew, sit down to eat it, and grade it in a prompt. Tasting your own food could be how a player finds their speciality. Maybe a coffee or an ale also restores spent cards. Odd, but there's something to it.

### Avatar shows cooking while waiting

The avatar window at the top changes to a cooking image while the keeper waits at a station.

### Lots of choice, and ways to organise it

If every garnish spot shows every garnish in the world, buying a new one makes your life harder. Choice needs sorting tools, such as stocking each station with a chosen set.

### Pipes and leaf

The keeper can sell pipes and tobacco products.

### Board games and tabletop games

The keeper can buy games and lay them out for guests.

### Skills are found, not bought

New things to do turn up in the world: you spot a snack bowl while buying supplies, and now snacks can go on the tables. Discovery pulls the player outward. Open: how to keep the essentials easy to find, so nobody misses something core (fits the never-feel-lost rule).

### Meal times, each with its own bill of fare

The player sets breakfast, lunch and dinner times, each with its own bill of fare. The hard part is keeping it easy. Suggestions:

- One all-day bill to start; splitting it into meals is optional, or comes later.
- Times are set on one strip of the day at the desk: drag the edges of each meal, snapping to the hour. It reads the shared time-of-day value.
- Each meal is a tab on the bill of fare, and a new meal starts as a copy of another.
- Bills carry over from day to day, so you only touch them when you want a change.
- In the world, a chalkboard by the door shows which meal is being served.

Decided: between meals there's no bill of fare, so guests can order only drinks and snacks. Guests already order only from what's on the bill, so the gap doesn't upset them. A guest who stays until a meal starts can order from that meal's bill.

Decided: no set opening hours for now. The player opens and closes the inn whenever they like, with nothing to set up. Set hours would turn into a duty, and breaking them would feel like losing something. Meals and their bills of fare are set on their own and apply whenever the inn happens to be open.

Later, maybe: set opening hours as an advanced option, a helper that opens and closes the inn for you, perhaps once there are employees for full service.

### Every guest has a name, learned over time

Guests first appear by role (farmhand, dockworker), but every one has a name. Maybe a keeper trait lets you know a guest by name after their first serving. A few named regulars still carry stories, so the taproom never fills up with "dockworker 1 to 7,000". Ties to #28.

Suggestion: learn names by default through familiarity (Spec 2: stranger, acquaintance, regular, friend), and let the trait speed it up to the first serving. Then no player is stuck with numbers.

Decided direction: learning names is dynamic. Some guests give their name easily, others take a long time. Suggestion: reuse each guest's openness (the same that makes regulars open about their tastes), so a chatty farmhand introduces himself on the first visit and a reserved one like Hedda takes weeks. Getting a guarded guest's name then becomes a small win.

## Setting and later ideas

### Setting tone for equipment

The fantasy that inspired Tolkien, the Inklings and C.S. Lewis, with a 1920s to 1950s charm, fantasy races and deliberate anachronisms (electricity arriving, a gramophone already there). Magic is a hint in the backdrop only. Equipment stays setting-aware: no microwaves, and electricity never steals the show.

### A roasting spit in the taproom (future)

A spit in the communal room, tended among the guests, for a very particular kind of tavern.

### Start on an empty plot (stretch goal)

Besides buying or inheriting a ready-made building that you license into configurations, you could buy an empty plot and start from nothing.

## Prototype fixes

### Readable chatter

Guest lines (weather, servings, story) have no background and overlap, and the top notices are hard to read too. Start with the first two steps:

1. Every speech line and top notice gets a dark, semi-transparent rounded plate; speech plates have a small tail pointing at the speaker.
2. One voice per table at a time: the next line waits until the current bubble fades. Bubbles from different tables nudge apart.
3. Later, priority when a queue builds: story and lines to the keeper first, dish reactions next, weather small talk last and dropped if the queue runs long.
4. Later, a chatter log in the side panel, filterable by table, so every line can be read.

### Numbered orders: daily tickets

Each order appears as a card aligned to the top bar, oldest on the left, showing the item and its number. The guest who ordered gets a matching plaque above their head; both go when the order is served.

These cards are in addition to the order list in the right-hand panel, which stays as it is (it works as a debug log for now).

Open: showing how long an order has waited. Bruno's idea: the card fills with red as it ages, or its border slowly turns red. Suggestion: the border ring sweeps round like a clock and warms from cream through amber to a soft red, without flooding the card, to keep it cozy ("never Overcooked").

Direction: guests leave if an order stays outstanding long enough. Suggestion: the ring then counts down to that walkout, so a full ring means they're about to go. It stays neutral for most of the wait and only warms in the last stretch, so it hints at a guest's patience without spelling it out.

Principle: the age signal does one job. It is readable at a glance, honest and accurate, and it warns when a customer is about to be lost; it doesn't encode anything more. Before designing it, check how the build already does this: tickets above guests' heads already turn red, but how that timer works isn't clear yet.

Layout and motion (decided):

- The row fills from the top left, oldest ticket leftmost. The snap point and the stack sit at the top right. A new ticket pops in at the snap point with a magnetic snap and a click, then slides left into the next free place.
- Tickets are never hidden. When the row is full, new tickets stack at the snap point, each newer one on top.
- Serving from the left: the row slides left to close the gap, and the stack feeds tickets back into the row.
- Serving out of order: that ticket snaps off, the row slides to close the gap, and the next ticket from the stack joins the end of the row.

Why: the oldest, most urgent orders sit next to the avatar, weather, date and time, so the important things are together on the left. The right side holds the debug log for now, which won't be in the game.

- Decided: daily tickets. Every order gets the next number (1, 2, 3…), up to three digits, reset each morning. A number is never reused within a day, so it only ever means one order.
- The last ticket doubles as the day's count of orders taken; it could appear in the journal at turn-in.
- Plaque: inspired by King's Cross platform signs. Round, thin dark outline, off-white fill, very dark grey numerals (about #222), in a high-contrast serif such as Playfair Display, Libre Caslon or EB Garamond.
